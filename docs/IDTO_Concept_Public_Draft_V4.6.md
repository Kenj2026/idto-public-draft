## 0. Executive Summary (TL;DR)

### The Problem
Technology increasingly defines social rules “as implementation,” but the perspectives (values, goals, and optimizations) driving that implementation are often invisible.

### The Goal
We need more than “regulation for punishment.” We need **public transparency infrastructure** that allows society to understand, verify, and improve systems.

### The Solution
IDTO is neither a regulator nor a rating agency. It proposes a **protocol (common language)** to make transparency **recordable and reviewable** worldwide.

### Vision (Adoption-first)
Our goal is to make this concept widely adoptable so that **anyone can implement it**.  
IDTO is **protocol-first** and implementation is **permissionless**: anyone can build tools, registries, or verification layers on top of a shared format.  
We aim for transparency to become a **shared public infrastructure**, not a centralized gatekeeper.

### 0.1 Fixed vs. Open Elements
**Fixed (Constitution)**
- Purpose: Shift digital decision-making from “impression” to “fact and verification.”
- Positioning: IDTO is a provider of a common language (protocol), not a judge.
- The **3 Pillars (Ch. 3)** & **6 Fixed Keywords (Ch. 4)**: non-negotiable core principles.

**Open (RFC: Request for Comments)**
- Operational details, granularity, incentives, and verification methods are intentionally open and gathered in **Chapter 8 (Future Challenges)** for structured discussion.

## 1. Introduction: Why IDTO?
Our society now relies on digital systems for everything—search, social media, payments, healthcare, and education. While convenient, technology is effectively “writing the law” through implementation. We often cannot see the “intent” behind these systems.

IDTO is a proposal for a **Public Infrastructure for Transparency** to bridge this gap:
- to make intent and change histories visible,
- to enable review and correction,
- and to shift accountability from “perception battles” to evidence-based improvement.

---

## 2. Ethical Foundation: “Justice” as Error-Correction
The core of justice in this draft is the ability to **correct errors**. No system is perfect. Reliability comes from the ability to discover, verify, and remediate mistakes.

IDTO aims to provide practical tools to return digital decision-making to a foundation of facts rather than impressions—by enabling structured disclosure, traceability, and review.

---

## 3. The Three Pillars (Conceptual Protocol)

### 3.1 Error-Correction as an OS
We do not assume perfection. Instead, we value systems that can be patched. The focus is not on “who is right,” but on “how we fix it.”

### 3.2 Recording “Intent & Change”
Every participant must connect at least two points:
- **Intent:** What is the system trying to achieve? (Optimization goals)
- **Change:** What was modified? (Change Logs)

This provides an objective evidence base to verify whether implementation matches intent.

### 3.3 Protocol, Not a Platform
IDTO is not a “walled garden.” It is a common language (protocol). We provide the “ground of facts” so that third parties can build their own analysis and visualization tools.

---

## 4. The 6 Fixed Keywords (Constitutional Level)
To avoid international misunderstanding, these six principles are fixed:

1) **Good-faith by default**: Designed for improvement, not punishment.  
2) **Non-regulatory**: No enforcement or sanctions. We provide the protocol.  
3) **Traceable & auditable**: Centered on Change Logs to enable learning (not punitive audits).  
4) **No rating**: No scoring or rankings. We stick to facts and procedures.  
5) **Federated**: No centralized data silos. Original data stays with the owner.  
6) **Politically neutral**: Providing the foundation for facts, not ideologies.

---

## 5. The “Three-Layer” Structure

### 5.1 IDTOpedia (Open Knowledge Base)
A collaborative encyclopedia explaining digital accountability based on evidence.

### 5.2 Open Disclosure Registry (Structured Ledger)
Machine-readable primary information (Intent, Data Handling, Change Logs).

In addition, to ensure transparency does not increase harm, the registry may include a **disclosure-level field** (e.g., public / limited / time-bound embargo) with a rationale and review date—so sensitive details can be staged without deleting history.

### 5.3 Verification Layer (Public Peer Review)
A layer where experts and the community review disclosures to ensure they are consistent and sufficient.

---

## 6. The Metaphor: “A README for Society”
Think of the digital world as a massive, complex source code. IDTO is the standard format for the README file. We do not write the content for everyone; we define the headers so that everyone else can read, verify, and update the “social code” in a way that everyone understands.

## 8. Future Challenges (RFC / Invitation to Discuss)

IDTO does not impose a “finished solution” from the start.  
What this draft aims to fix is the **purpose**, the **positioning** (non-regulatory / no-rating / protocol-first), and a **minimal common language** that enables transparency to be recordable and reviewable.

Operational design, interoperability mechanisms, verification methods, and adoption pathways must be refined iteratively across jurisdictions and domains.  
This chapter intentionally lists unresolved issues—not to declare “the answer,” but to align discussion focus and reduce misunderstanding and talking-past-each-other costs.

### 8.1 Independence: How do we protect IDTO from capture?
- What does “independence” prioritize (governance, records, funding, verification)?
- Independence from whom (states, corporations, donors, ideologies)?
- Where is the boundary between IDTO’s scope and other systems (law, contracts, audits, whistleblower protections)?

### 8.2 Change Governance: Who can change the protocol itself?
- What qualifies as a “change” (schema, definitions, operations, constitution-level wording, funding rules)?
- What are the approval requirements (who, how many, how transparent, how long)?
- How should emergency changes (security incidents, legal requirements) be handled and post-reviewed?

### 8.3 Traceability Specs: How do we standardize Change Logs across domains?
- What is the minimal standard unit of traceability (Intent, Decision, Change, Release, etc.)?
- How should disclosure scope be recorded (public / limited / non-public) and justified?
- How do we implement “correction without deletion” (superseding logs rather than erasing history)?
- How do we connect auditability to learning and improvement (not punishment)?

### 8.4 Federated Operations: How do we ensure interoperability without centralizing power?
- Minimum participation requirements (technical, ethical, conflict-of-interest, commitment to principles)
- What must be shared vs. what can remain local (minimal verifiability core)
- Join/leave/suspension processes that prevent abuse while preserving recoverability
- Mapping strategy to existing registries and schemas (“do not replace existing efforts”)

### 8.5 Funding & Neutrality: How do we maintain financial independence over time?
- Funding diversity, caps, sponsor disclosure, handling conditional funding
- Financial transparency granularity (what is published, how often, in what audit form)
- How to prevent funding from becoming governance control (guardrails and separation)

### 8.6 Privacy & Safety: How do we ensure transparency does not increase harm?
- Handling personal data, sensitive data, national security concerns, and trade secrets
- Cases where publishing logs increases risk (retaliation, harassment, doxxing, exploitation)
- Alignment strategy with jurisdictional rules (data protection, whistleblowing, archiving duties)

### 8.7 Defensive Disclosure & Safe-Harbor-by-Protocol (RFC)
Honest disclosure can increase legal, reputational, and security risk—creating incentives for vague, minimal, or delayed disclosure (“defensive disclosure”).  
IDTO cannot grant legal immunity; however, it may standardize **evidence formats** for good-faith disclosure and remediation that external systems can consider.

Open questions:
- Can we standardize staged disclosure (public / limited / time-bound embargo) with rationale, review dates, and clear un-embargo criteria?
- Can we define “safety redaction logs” (what was withheld, why, and when it will be reviewed) without deleting history?
- What machine-readable evidence of good-faith remediation should be recorded (discover → mitigate → patch → verify → prevent recurrence)?
- How can external ecosystems (contracts, insurance, procurement, compliance) consume such evidence without IDTO becoming a regulator or a judge?

### 8.8 Incentives & Trust Signals (without rating) (RFC)
IDTO is strictly **no-rating**: no scores, rankings, or centralized judgments.  
At the same time, if honest disclosure is always a cost, adoption will stall. The question is how to make transparency economically viable without turning IDTO into a rating or compliance regime.

Open questions:
- Instead of a single score, what **descriptive metadata** can indicate disclosure “health” (update frequency, changelog granularity, response times to verification, correction cadence)?
- Can communities develop domain-specific “exemplary README / disclosure templates” as best practices without centralized certification?
- How should interoperability with external decision-makers be designed (export formats, URIs, signatures, update tracking), while IDTO itself makes no judgments?

### 8.9 Machine Readability & Structured Schemas: How far should we formalize? (RFC)
IDTO currently prioritizes human-readable narratives to enable broad adoption.  
However, stronger machine-readable structure could unlock automated consistency checks, diff-based audits, and scalable verification.

Tension:
- More structure improves automation and consistency.
- More structure increases authoring cost and may reduce adoption.

Options to discuss (non-exclusive):
- (A) Staged schema maturity: Minimal / Standard / Advanced  
  Minimal: small required field set + narrative  
  Standard: structured change log + key metadata + clear mappings  
  Advanced: formal validation rules, typed events, signatures
- (B) JSON Schema (or equivalent) as a reference specification (types, required fields, enumerations, dependencies)
- (C) Event-style Change Logs (event sourcing): changes recorded as typed events with timestamps and scopes
- (D) Lightweight constraints (if-then rules) to detect contradictions without becoming a compliance system

Open questions:
- Which options deliver the highest value with the lowest adoption cost?
- What is the minimal structured set required to make verification “real” (not only rhetorical)?
- How should narrative and structured fields divide responsibilities?

### 8.10 Verification Quality, Neutrality & Community Norms (RFC)
A verification layer is only valuable if it produces constructive improvement rather than polarization or capture.  
At the same time, “neutrality” is difficult: it requires explicit norms, processes, and transparency of reviewers.

Open questions:
- What neutrality standard should guide verification behavior (e.g., an NPOV-like principle adapted for disclosures)?
- What minimum review format converts critique into patches (claim → evidence → impact → proposed patch → alternatives)?
- How should conflicts of interest be disclosed and managed in verification discussions?
- What conduct norms are required to prevent harassment, doxxing, and extractive “gotcha” dynamics?
- What moderation and escalation model is lightweight enough for early adoption but robust against abuse?
